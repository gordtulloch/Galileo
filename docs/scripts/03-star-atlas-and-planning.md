# Episode 3: Star Atlas and Planning

Target running time: 30 minutes. Lines beginning with `>` are production directions and are not narrated.

## 1. Opening

> SCREEN: Star Atlas, wide view of the Milky Way.

Welcome back. This episode is about deciding what to image, and when. We'll explore the Star Atlas, rank tonight's targets, search the deep-sky catalog, keep a target list and compose the shot. Carrying the plan out, with sessions and scheduling, has its own episode.

None of this needs equipment connected, so it's an ideal cloudy-night activity. It does need your Observatory's latitude and longitude, so set those first, as shown in episode two.

## 2. The Star Atlas layout

> SCREEN: Highlight the left panel, the sky map, and the right panel.

The Star Atlas is a live planetarium of the sky over your site. On the left are the time, location and display options. In the centre is the sky. On the right are details of whatever you've selected, with buttons to act on it.

Drag to pan. Scroll to zoom. Click an object to identify it. Double-click to centre on it and keep tracking it.

> SCREEN: Pan, zoom into Orion, click M 42, double-click.

Behind the stars is a real all-sky photograph of the Milky Way by Axel Mellinger. Tiles download as they come into view and are kept on your computer, so what you've already visited is available offline. You can turn it off with Sky survey.

## 3. Time and place

> SCREEN: Time controls. Step forward by hours.

With Live ticked, the map follows your clock. Edit the time, or use the step buttons to move by an hour or a day, and Live turns off so the sky stays where you put it. This is an easy way to see when something rises, or to preview next week.

> SCREEN: Step forward six hours and back, then tick Live again.

Latitude and longitude come from the Observatory, but you can edit them here to look from somewhere else, without changing your saved site.

## 5. Your horizon

> SCREEN: Options, Star Atlas, horizon table and Upload horizon file.

Most of us have trees or a house in the way. Describe them once and Galileo takes them into account.

Under Options, Star Atlas, you'll find a horizon table. Each row is a compass direction and the height of the obstruction in that direction. Upload a text file with one pair per line, or add points by hand, then press Save Changes. Each Observatory keeps its own horizon.

## 6. Finding and choosing a target

> SCREEN: Type "Veil" in the Find box and press Enter.

Type a name or catalog number in Find and press Enter. Everything that might match is plotted in yellow, however faint, even from a catalog that's switched off. The best match is centred and selected, and you can click any of the others instead.

> PAUSE: 22

The panel on the right shows the name, type, magnitude, constellation, coordinates, altitude and azimuth.

Selecting an object makes it the Pier's current object. The top bar shows it on every screen. Frames you capture are named after it, the Solve screen can slew back to it, and What's Up Tonight pins it. It's kept for the current run of Galileo, and each Pier has its own.

> SCREEN: Highlight the current object in the top bar.

## 7. Acting from the atlas

> SCREEN: Right-click an object to show the menu.

With a mount connected, you can act directly from the sky. Goto slews to the selected object. Goto and Centre slews, then takes a picture, plate solves it and corrects the pointing, using the settings on the Solve screen. Sync tells the mount that it's pointing at this object. Add to Session starts a new session with a slew to this target already in it. Park or unpark is there too. You can also right-click empty sky and go to that spot.

> SCREEN: Goto an object and watch the telescope marker move.

> PAUSE: 31

Save to Targets keeps a star or deep-sky object for later. Saved targets are always drawn on the map in orange, and they also appear in the mobile apps. Planets, comets, asteroids and satellites can't be saved, because they move.

## 8. What's Up Tonight

> SCREEN: Planning, What's Up Tonight.

Now open Planning. The first page is What's Up Tonight, which ranks deep-sky objects for a night at your site, best first.

The ranking considers how high each object gets and for how long, how close it is to the Moon, whether it fits your telescope's field of view, the weather forecast, aurora activity and smoke, and how much you've already imaged it.

Set the observing date, which defaults to tonight, the faintest magnitude to consider, the minimum altitude and the catalogs. Then press Rank Tonight.

> SCREEN: Set criteria and press Rank Tonight.

> PAUSE: 28

Across the top is a day and night band from noon to noon, showing day, the three kinds of twilight, and true night, with their times. Deep-sky imaging is best in the night segment.

Below it are advisories for tonight: weather, aurora and smoke. These need an internet connection and apply to today only.

If you have an Astrospheric Professional membership, enter your key under Options, Planning, and you'll get hour by hour rows of cloud, seeing, transparency, temperature, dew point and wind, lined up under the night band, plus smoke and aurora rows that need no key. Astrospheric covers North America, Greenland, Iceland, Ireland and the United Kingdom.

## 9. Reading the result tiles

> SCREEN: Close-up of one tile.

Each tile shows the rank, name, type, brightness, size, constellation, best altitude, and the reasons for its score. If you already have data on it, you'll see the hours you've collected. A Reduced confidence badge means some input, such as your horizon or the forecast, wasn't available; hover over it to see which.

Thumbnails appear only if they're already on your computer. To get them all, use Download Catalog Images under Options, Planning. It's one large download, it can be cancelled, and afterwards pictures work offline, including the enlarged view.

> SCREEN: Click a thumbnail to enlarge it.

> PAUSE: 22

From a tile you can Save it to Targets, Select it as the current object, Slew To it, Goto and Centre, or Add to Session.

## 13. Choosing a good target

> SCREEN: What's Up Tonight beside the Star Atlas.

The tools give you numbers. Here's how to read them. Altitude matters most. Low down you look through much more air, so stars are soft and faint detail washes out. Try to catch your object while it's high, ideally near its highest as it crosses the meridian.

> PAUSE: 19

Then consider the Moon. A bright Moon nearby drowns faint galaxies and reflection nebulae. Clusters, and emission nebulae through narrowband filters, cope much better.

Then size. A large nebula on a long telescope needs a mosaic. A tiny planetary nebula on a short lens will be a dot. The field of view overlay in the atlas answers this at a glance.

> SCREEN: Compare the field of view on a large and a small object.

> PAUSE: 31

Finally, time. A faint object needs many hours. The hours integrated on a tile show what you already have, so you can choose to deepen last week's data instead of starting something new.

## 15. Looking ahead to other nights

Change the observing date to plan a future night, perhaps a weekend near new Moon. The sky is recomputed for that date. Weather, aurora and smoke are only available for today, so those scores carry reduced confidence, which the badge tells you.

## 16. A little sky vocabulary

> SCREEN: Atlas with the coordinate grid on.

If you're newer to this, two coordinate systems appear all over these screens. Right ascension and declination are fixed to the stars, like longitude and latitude on the sky. They're how an object's address is written. Altitude and azimuth are local: how high above your horizon, and which compass direction. Those change minute by minute as the Earth turns.

> PAUSE: 19

The meridian is the line from north to south passing overhead. An object is highest when it crosses it. That's the best time to image it, and also the moment a German equatorial mount has to think about flipping.

> SCREEN: Step time forward and watch an object cross the meridian.

> PAUSE: 31

Twilight comes in three stages. In civil twilight it's still quite bright. In nautical twilight the brighter stars are out. In astronomical twilight the sky looks dark to your eye, but not yet to a long exposure. True night begins after that. At high latitudes in summer, true night may not happen at all, and the day and night band will show you so.

> PAUSE: 16

## 17. Measuring your own horizon

> SCREEN: Phone compass app, then the horizon table.

A good horizon file is worth an evening. Stand where the telescope stands, at about its height. Work around the compass. At each obstacle, note the direction with a compass and the height with a phone inclinometer, or point the telescope at the edge of the obstacle and read its altitude and azimuth from the Mount page.

> PAUSE: 25

Add a point at each corner, such as both edges of a roofline, since points are joined by straight lines. Remember that a compass reads magnetic north, so correct it to true north, or use the telescope method. Save, turn on Horizon in the atlas, and compare the red shape with what you see. If you have two sites, each Observatory has its own.

> PAUSE: 25

## 19. If something looks wrong

> SCREEN: Observatory settings dialog.

If the sky looks wrong, check latitude, longitude and the sign of each. West is negative. Check your computer's time and time zone. If objects you expect are missing, check which catalogs and magnitude limits are on. If there are no pictures on the tiles, download the catalog images. And if a slew is refused, look for the reason in the status bar: usually a parked mount, a mount limit or your horizon.
